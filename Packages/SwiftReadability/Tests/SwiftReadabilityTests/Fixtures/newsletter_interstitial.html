Growing Tomatoes in Containers

Container tomatoes reward a gardener who has no bed to dig: a five-gallon pot, a sunny step, and a determined vine are enough to carry a plant from seedling to fruit. The trick most beginners miss is that a container dries out far faster than open ground, so the difference between a thriving plant and a wilted one is usually just the watering can.

Choose a determinate variety bred for pots rather than one bred for a trellised row; the plant will stay compact and still set a heavy crop by midsummer. Bury the stem deeper than it grew in its nursery cell, since tomatoes root readily along a buried stem and the extra roots make a sturdier plant later on.

Feed a container plant more often than one in open soil, since every watering also washes nutrients out through the drainage holes. A balanced liquid feed every two weeks keeps the leaves dark green and the trusses setting fruit steadily through the season rather than stalling in August.

Stake or cage the plant early, before the first truss sets, because a tomato loaded with green fruit is far heavier than the seedling that went into the pot and a stake driven in later risks tearing through roots that have already filled the container.

By the first frost warning, a container tomato has usually given most of what it will give for the year; pulling the whole plant indoors to ripen its last green fruit on a windowsill is the one harvest trick worth remembering season to season.
