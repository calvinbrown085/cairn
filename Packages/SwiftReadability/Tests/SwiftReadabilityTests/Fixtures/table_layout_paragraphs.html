Every vessel that leaves harbor is required to keep a log, and every officer who has stood a watch knows that the requirement is the least interesting part of the practice. The log exists first as a legal record, a defense against every later question about who did what and when, but it survives in memory for a different reason entirely: it is the only place aboard a ship where the ordinary hours get written down at all.

Most of what happens on a long passage is not dramatic enough to mention to anyone waiting at the other end of the voyage. Wind shifts two points, a sail gets trimmed, a bird that should not be this far from land lands on the rail and stays for an hour before continuing wherever it was going. None of it is a story by itself, and yet a log kept faithfully across a month of such entries becomes a different kind of document than any single day could produce, because it is the accumulation that carries the information, not any one entry in isolation.

There is a discipline to writing the log that has nothing to do with literary ambition and everything to do with reliability under fatigue. An officer coming off a difficult watch at four in the morning does not want to compose a paragraph; the format has to be forgiving enough that a tired hand can still fill it in correctly, and specific enough that thirty years of entries from different hands remain comparable to one another. That tension, between what is easy to write at the worst hour and what is useful to read decades later, shapes almost every convention the form has settled into.

Reading old logs now, the entries that stand out are rarely the ones recording storms or near misses. Those get written up elsewhere, retold at length, embellished a little each time they are told again. It is the flat entries, the ones logging an unremarkable noon position on an unremarkable day, that carry the most information about what the ordinary texture of a voyage was actually like, because nobody thought to preserve that texture any other way. The document that was kept purely out of obligation turns out, much later, to be the only faithful record of the parts of the trip that nobody meant to remember on purpose.

This is, in miniature, the case for keeping any kind of ordinary record at all: not because any single entry matters, but because the habit of writing plainly and regularly produces an archive that no deliberate act of memory could reconstruct after the fact, and there is no way to start that archive later and have it cover the years already gone.
