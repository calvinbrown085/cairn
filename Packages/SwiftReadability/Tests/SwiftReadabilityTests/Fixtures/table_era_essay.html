June 2007

A couple weeks ago I had a thought so heretical that it's taken me till now to work up the nerve to write about it: that maybe the way to get more done, at least for a certain kind of person, is not to try to do more, but to be willing to do less.

I've felt this coming for a while. There's a kind of pressure inside every ambitious person to be seen visibly grinding, and I think for a lot of people that pressure is actively bad for the work. What looks like slack to an observer is often the space where the interesting idea shows up on its own schedule instead of on a deadline. That doesn't mean laziness is a virtue; it means the appearance of constant effort is not the same thing as the substance of good work, and the two get confused more often than people admit.

I remember a specific afternoon working on a piece of software that was giving me trouble. I'd been staring at the same function for an hour, moving pieces around, not getting anywhere, and finally gave up and went for a walk instead of grinding through lunch. Twenty minutes into the walk the actual shape of the fix arrived, fully formed, in a way that no amount of additional staring at the editor would have produced. This has happened often enough now that I no longer treat it as luck; it's closer to how the work actually gets done for people wired a certain way, and treating it as a scheduling problem instead of a willpower problem changed how I plan a week.

The naive fix is to schedule more walks, but that misses the point in the same way that scheduling more brainstorming misses the point of a good idea. The walk isn't the mechanism; the absence of forced output is the mechanism, and a walk is just one shape that absence happens to take. For someone else it might be a long shower, a drive with no destination, or the twenty minutes between waking up and actually getting out of bed. What all of them share is that nobody is watching, so there's nothing to perform.

None of this is an argument for doing less work in the aggregate. It's an argument that the ratio of visible grinding to actual output is not fixed, and that pushing on the wrong side of that ratio can make both numbers worse at once. The people who seem unhurried are not necessarily doing less; they have often just stopped confusing the appearance of effort with the thing effort is supposed to produce.
