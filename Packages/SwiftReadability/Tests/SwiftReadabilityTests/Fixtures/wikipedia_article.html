The giant sequoia (Sequoiadendron giganteum; giant redwood, Sierra redwood, Sierran redwood, California big tree) is the sole living species in the genus Sequoiadendron, and one of three species of coniferous trees known as redwoods.[1] Giant sequoia specimens are the most massive individual trees in the world, and the species is among the oldest living things on Earth, with the oldest confirmed specimen dated at around 3,266 years.[2]

The giant sequoia is native to a narrow band of mixed conifer forest, 260 km long by 24 km wide, on the western slopes of the Sierra Nevada in California, at altitudes of 1,400 to 2,150 metres. It is protected in a series of national parks and reserves, including Sequoia National Park and Yosemite National Park, and is also cultivated as an ornamental tree well beyond its natural range.[3] Its bark is fibrous, furrowed, and can be exceptionally thick, sometimes exceeding sixty centimetres at the base of the largest trunks, which affords the species considerable resistance to fire damage.

Description[edit]

Giant sequoias grow to an average height of 50 to 85 metres and have a trunk diameter of 6 to 8 metres. Record trees have been measured at 94.8 metres tall and with a diameter of 17 metres. The oldest known giant sequoia, based on ring count, is 3,266 years old.[4] The foliage is scale-like, bright green in colour, and arranged in a spiral pattern along slender branchlets, quite unlike the flattened sprays typical of its coastal cousin.

Because of the tree's size, giant sequoia wood might be expected to have been a valuable resource, but the great majority of felled trees shattered on impact owing to their brittleness, and huge quantities of usable timber were left to rot after early logging operations proved commercially disappointing. This early recognition of waste, more than aesthetic appreciation, drove some of the first conservation efforts on the tree's behalf in the nineteenth century.

Ecology[edit]

Giant sequoias require a very particular combination of environmental factors for successful reproduction, a fact not fully appreciated by land managers until the middle of the twentieth century. Fire plays an essential ecological role: it clears competing vegetation, opens the forest canopy to let in the light seedlings need, and dries the cones enough to release their seeds onto the mineral soil that new sequoias require to take root. Decades of fire suppression in the parks that protect the species inadvertently reduced its ability to reproduce, a problem land managers now address through prescribed and managed burns.[5]

The trees are also remarkably resistant to insect damage and fungal decay, owing to high concentrations of tannin in the bark and heartwood, which helps explain why fallen trunks can remain largely intact on the forest floor for centuries after they topple.
