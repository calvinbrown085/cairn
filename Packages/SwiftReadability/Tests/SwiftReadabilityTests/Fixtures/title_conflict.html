The Starter Nobody Told You About

A sourdough starter is nothing more than flour and water left to catch whatever wild yeast and bacteria happen to be drifting through the kitchen air, and once that culture takes hold it will keep itself alive for decades with almost no attention beyond a regular meal of more flour and water.

Most starters fail in the first two weeks not because the culture is weak but because the baker panics at the smell: a starter goes through an acetone-like phase as the yeast and bacteria populations sort themselves out, and feeding it more often during that phase, not throwing it away, is what carries it through to the other side.

Once a starter is established, it wants a rhythm more than a recipe: the same rough feeding ratio and the same rough interval every time trains the culture to peak predictably, which matters far more to the bread's rise than any particular flour blend or water temperature you read about in a forum thread.

A starter kept in the refrigerator can coast on a once-a-week feeding for months, which is the only reason a home baker without a daily bread habit can keep one going at all; pulled out and fed twice before a bake, it wakes back up to full strength within a day.

The oldest continuously maintained starters on record are well over a century old, passed down and divided among bakers the way a sourdough culture always has been, which is as much of a guarantee of its resilience as any laboratory study could offer a nervous first-time keeper.
